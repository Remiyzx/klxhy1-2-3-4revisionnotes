2. The "Y & W" Bodyguards (Zero Initial Syllables) 🛡️

KLXHY Pg 4

Only big-mouth vowels a, o, e can stand alone (ā, è). Vowels i, u, ü require Y or W bodyguards:

i Group:
Alone → add y (yi, yin, ying)
Mixed → replace i with y (ia→ya, ian→yan)
u Group:
Alone → add w (u→wu)
Mixed → replace u with w (ua→wa, uan→wan)
ü Group:
Always add y AND drop two dots (ü→yu, üe→yue, üan→yuan, ün→yun)

4. Who Gets the Tone Crown? 👑

KLXHY Pg 5

Priority ladder based on vowel mouth opening:

a > o > e > i > u > ü
Rule 1: Single Vowel Mark directly on it: mā, bǐ, kù
Rule 2: Two Vowels Follow ladder priority: bǎi, guó, piào
Rule 3: Twin Twins (iu & ui) Crown sits on the LAST vowel: tuī, qiú
Rule 4: Three Vowels Crown sits on the MIDDLE vowel: guǎi, shuài
Rule 5: Dot-Free "i" Erase dot when crowned: nǐ, yín
Rule 6: Neutral Tone No mark on neutral syllables: bàba, māma

5. The Syllable Slicer / Dividing Mark (') ✂️

Official Term: Dividing Mark. Used when a syllable starting with a, o, e follows another syllable, preventing syllable boundary confusion:

Tiananmen → Tiān'ānmén

6. Word Glue & Capitalization ✍️

Syllables forming one meaningful word are glued together with no space. Capitalize sentence beginnings and proper nouns:

wǒ de peng you → Wǒ de péngyou.

2. Chinese Characters (Hànzì)

Strokes, Writing Mechanics, Confusing Strokes & Stroke Order KLXHY Pg 8-13

Core Principle: Stroke & Character Concept

In Chinese writing, we ONLY learn strokes and characters. A stroke is the fundamental building block of a character, written in ONE single continuous movement from start to finish without lifting your pen from the paper!

Master Stroke Classification

Strokes with Turns (zhé) (3)

KLXHY Pg 8, 10
💡 Defining "zhé" & The Naming Secret:

• Definition & Inseparability: A turn (zhé) is part of the stroke itself and never an independent stroke! It is a continuous change of direction without lifting your pen (not strictly a 90° corner, as piě zhé forms an acute/slanted turn!).
• Why is "héng zhé" NOT called "héng shù"? Why is "shù zhé" NOT called "shù héng"?
Because a stroke is defined as ONE continuous movement without lifting pen from paper. Calling it "héng shù" would make it sound like two separate strokes. Because the stroke turns mid-way in a single action, that turning action itself is named zhé (Turn)! Hence: héng + zhé = héng zhé.
• Writing Rule Angle: In héng zhé, after writing the horizontal line, the turn MUST proceed top to bottom. In shù zhé, after writing the vertical line, the turn MUST proceed left to right. Reason: Chinese character stroke rules dictate that we NEVER write strokes from bottom to top or from right to left (with the sole exception of tí)!

Stroke NameSymbolDirection & Writing Technique
héng zhé𠃍Horizontal line turning strictly top to bottom without lifting pen.
shù zhé𠃊Vertical line turning strictly left to right without lifting pen.
piě zhé𠃋Slanted down-left then turning into a sharp horizontal right stroke.

Strokes with Hooks (gōu) (6)

💡 Important Note on Hook Variations:

A gōu (hook) is part of the stroke itself and never an independent stroke! (Both zhé and gōu are single continuous movements). Furthermore, xié gōu, wān gōu, and wò gōu are all variations/mutations originating from shù gōu! A hook does not have one rigid fixed direction—it simply follows the natural stroke flow to execute the flick!

Stroke NameSymbolDirection & Writing Technique
héng gōu乛Horizontal line ending with a short, sharp flick down-left.
shù gōu亅Straight vertical stem ending with a sharp hook flicking up-left.
xié gōu㇂"xié" sounds like senget (slanted). Slanted stem falling down-right, hook points straight up to 12 o'clock.
wò gōu㇃"wò" means sleep / lie down; the stroke feels like it is completely lying down with the hook pointing inward.
wān gōu㇁"wān" means curve; softens the rigid straight vertical line into a gentle curved belly before hooking up-left.
shù wān gōu乚Vertical down, glides across a smooth flat bottom, then hooks straight up.

Complex Strokes (5)

Stroke NameSymbolDirection & Writing Technique
héng zhé tí㇊Horizontal → turn down → lift up-right.
héng zhé gōu𠃌Horizontal → turn down → hook left.
shù zhé zhé gōu㇉Down → right → down → hook left with sharp corners.
héng xié gōu⺄Horizontal → slant down-right → hook up.
héng zhé wān gōu㇈Horizontal → turn down → smooth curved base → hook up.

👁️ Spot the Differences: All 5 Confusing Stroke Pairs!

1. Straight vs Slanted vs Curved Hooks:
• shù gōu (亅): Straight vertical stem, hooks up-left.
• xié gōu (㇂): Slanted diagonal stem, hook points straight up to 12 o'clock.
• wān gōu (㇁): Curving right-bracket belly shape, hooks up-left.
2. Sharp Turn vs Wide Angle:
• piě zhé (𠃋): Acute turning corner, ends with flat horizontal line.
• piě diǎn (𡿨): Wide angle, second segment continues falling down-right as a long dot.
3. Left Hook vs Right Lift:
• shù gōu (亅): Hook flicks to the LEFT.
• shù tí (𠄌): Lift flicks up to the RIGHT like striking a match!
4. Lift Up-Right vs Hook Down-Left:
• héng zhé tí (㇊): Turns down and then LIFTS up-right.
• héng zhé gōu (𠃌): Turns down and then HOOKS inward to the left.
5. Straight Slant vs Curved Flat Base:
• héng xié gōu (⺄): Slants diagonally straight down-right before hooking up.
• héng zhé wān gōu (㇈): Turns down, glides across a smooth curved bottom base, then hooks up.

8 Rules for Writing Full Characters

KLXHY Pg 9
  • 1. Horizontal before vertical (十, 木)
  • 2. Left-falling before right-falling (人, 大)
  • 3. From top to bottom (三, 劳)
  • 4. From left to right (川, 什)
  • 5. From outside to inside (月, 问)
  • 6. Fill inside before sealing frame (四, 国)
  • 7. Middle precedes the two sides (小, 水)
  • 8. Vertical passing middle comes last (手, 事)

Character Structure Types

KLXHY Pg 9
  • Single-Component: One solid unit (木, 马, 人, 月).
  • Left-Right: Two side-by-side parts (好, 你, 认, 朋).
  • Top-Bottom: Stacked parts (字, 早, 点, 学).
  • Triangle: Pyramid structure (品, 森).
  • Semi-Enclosed: 2 or 3 sides framed (进, 店, 问, 医, 句).
  • Fully Enclosed: All 4 sides surrounded (国, 四, 回, 园).

How to Say "2" in Chinese? (èr vs liǎng)

èr
  • • The number itself for simple counting: yī, èr, sān...
  • • Mathematics equations & ID numbers
  • • Ordinals (second floor: èr céng)
  • • Tens and ones places: shí'èr (12), èrshí (20)
liǎng
  • • Used when indicating quantity with a Measure Word (e.g., liǎng ge rén).
  • ⚠️ The total quantity measured must strictly be only 2!
  • • In currency units: liǎng kuài (RM 2)
  • • In time hours: liǎng diǎn (2:00)

The Universal Chinese Number Formula

Number + Unit / Measure Word + Noun
[ Nu + M + N ]
🚨 CRITICAL EXCEPTION: ONLY Days of the Week do NOT follow this format!

📅 Days of the Week: xīngqī + Nu

Days of the week use xīngqī + Nu (e.g. xīngqīyī = Monday). Using Nu + xīngqī indicates the quantity of weeks (e.g. yī xīngqī = one week)!

• Monday to Saturday: xīngqīyī → xīngqīliù
• Sunday (Special Exception): xīngqītiān (spoken) / xīngqīrì (formal/written)
📌 Out of Syllabus (Extra Knowledge):
• Months (1-12): Nu + yuè (yīyuè = Jan, èryuè = Feb, ..., shí'èryuè = Dec)
• Dates (1-31): Nu + hào (spoken) / Nu + rì (formal) (yī hào = 1st, shíwǔ hào = 15th, sānshíyī hào = 31st)

1. Authentic Everyday Chinese Greetings Culture

"Nǐ hǎo" vs "Nǐ hǎo ma?" & The Copy-and-Paste Rule

• You ONLY answer with "Wǒ hěn hǎo" if you hear the question particle "ma" (Nǐ hǎo ma?)!

• This Copy & Paste rule applies across all greetings:

2. The "hěn" Rule & "yě" Sequence (S + adv + A)

Deep Dive: Why do we say "Wǒ hěn hǎo" instead of "Wǒ hǎo"?

In Chinese syntax, saying an adjective directly after a subject without any adverb (e.g. saying Wǒ hǎo, Tā máng, or Nǐ měi) sounds grammatically incomplete and carries a sharp, unintended comparative contrast. It implies an unspoken comparison (e.g. "I am good [while you are not]" or answering a selective question "Who is doing well? - I am")!

Therefore, in an ordinary, descriptive statement, Chinese grammar requires a neutral degree adverb—most fundamentally hěn—to act as an essential grammatical stabilizer and bridge. In this role, hěn loses its heavy literal meaning of "very"; it simply creates a well-balanced, polite predicate meaning "I'm doing fine"!

Word Order: "yě" precedes "hěn" (S + yě + hěn + A)

Simple intuitive rule: hěn glues directly to the adjective to form one single description unit ([hěn hǎo] = doing fine). yě (also) applies to the whole person, so it must stand in front:

Wǒ yě hěn hǎo.  (Never: Wǒ hěn yě hǎo ❌)

Subject (S) Adverb 1 (Scope) Adverb 2 (Degree Bridge) Adjective (A)
Wǒ / Tā / Lǎoshī yě (also) hěn (structural bridge) hǎo (good / fine)

3. Chinese Basic Sentence Structure (S + V + O & S + adv + A)

💡 NO Verb Conjugations in Chinese! (Past vs Present vs Future)

Unlike English, which constantly changes verb forms (eat / ate / eaten), Chinese verbs NEVER change form! Tenses are indicated simply by adding time words:

Yesterday: Wǒ zuótiān qù túshūguǎn.
Today: Wǒ jīntiān qù túshūguǎn.
Tomorrow: Wǒ míngtiān qù túshūguǎn.
Questions Follow the Exact Same Word Order:

In Chinese, statements, yes-no questions with ma, and 5W1H questions follow the exact same S + V + O word order!

• Statement: Tā qù cāntīng. (He goes to the restaurant.)
• Yes/No Question: Tā qù cāntīng ma? (Is he going to the restaurant?)
• 5W1H Question: Tā qù nǎr? (Where is he going?)
• Statement: Nǐ chī hànbāo. (You eat hamburgers.)
• Yes/No Question: Nǐ chī hànbāo ma? (Do you eat hamburgers?)
• 5W1H Question: Nǐ chī shénme? (What do you eat?)
🚨 GOLDEN RULE: NEVER Combine 5W1H Words with "ma"!

Since 5W1H words like nǎr / nǎlǐ (where) and shénme (what) already turn the sentence into a question, DO NOT attach "ma" at the end!

✓ Correct: Nǐ qù nǎr?  |  Nǐ chī shénme?
✗ Wrong: Nǐ qù nǎr ma?  |  Nǐ chī shénme ma?

4. Adverbs Modifying Verbs: S + adv + V + O

Position of Adverbs ("bù" and "yě") Before Verbs:

In Chinese, adverbs like bù (not/no) and yě (also) MUST be placed directly in front of the verb. When both appear together, yě always comes before bù (yě bù / yě bú = also not):

Subject (S) Adverb (adv) Verb (V) Object (O)
Wǒ bù chī hànbāo. (I don't eat burgers)
Tā yě qù dàxué. (He also goes to uni)
Wǒmen yě bú qù cāntīng. (We also don't go to canteen)

5. Question Particles: ma vs ne

ma - Yes-No Question

Simply take any normal statement and add "ma?" at the very end to turn it into a yes-no question!

Nǐ zài dàxué ma? (Are you at the university?)

ne - Bounce-Back Question

The "What about you?" shortcut! Instead of repeating the entire sentence, just say [Person] + ne? to bounce the same question right back:

Wǒ hěn hǎo, nǐ ne? (I'm fine, what about you?)

6. Tone Sandhi Rules for "bù"

Textbook Pg 36
Meaning of "bù": "bù" means "no" or "not", placed directly in front of verbs or adjectives to make them negative.
Normal 4th Tone (bù): Before 1st, 2nd, and 3rd tone syllables:
• bù chī (1st tone)
• bù huí (2nd tone)
• bù xǐhuan (3rd tone)
Changes to 2nd Tone (bú): Before another 4th tone syllable:
• bú zài (4th tone)
• bú qù (4th tone)
• bú yào (4th tone)
• bú kèqi (4th tone)
V-not-V Neutral Tone (bu): In choice questions, "bu" is light & neutral:
• yào bu yào
• chī bu chī
• qù bu qù

7. Time Word Position: S + Time + V + O or Time + S + V + O

In Chinese, time words MUST be placed BEFORE the verb. They can comfortably sit in two slots: EITHER directly after the Subject (S + Time + V + O) OR before the Subject (Time + S + V + O)! Hierarchical order: LARGEST unit to SMALLEST unit (Day → Time of Day → Clock Time):

Progressive Time Expansion (Big to Small):
1. Wǒ míngtiān qù túshūguǎn. (I am going to the library tomorrow.)
2. Wǒ míngtiān zǎoshang qù túshūguǎn. (I am going to the library tomorrow morning.)
3. Wǒ míngtiān zǎoshang bā diǎn qù túshūguǎn. (...tomorrow morning at 8:00.)
4. Wǒ míngtiān zǎoshang bā diǎn sānshí fēn qù túshūguǎn. (...tomorrow morning at 8:30.)

8. Place Word Position: S + Time + Place (V + Location) + V + O

Chinese Spatial Grammar Logic: Establish Location First!

In Chinese grammar logic, you must physically move to or be present at a location before performing an action there! Therefore, the place phrase (V [qù / huí / zài] + Location) MUST stand BEFORE the secondary action verb:

Formula: Subject + (Time) + [qù / huí / zài + Location] + Action Verb + Object
Or: (Time) + Subject + [qù / huí / zài + Location] + Action Verb + Object
