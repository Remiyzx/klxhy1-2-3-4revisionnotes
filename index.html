📐 How to Count Chinese Syllables & Words:

In Chinese, 1 Hanzi Character = 1 Spoken Syllable = 1 Pinyin Unit. Every single syllable you speak counts as exactly 1 syllable, regardless of how many letters are used to spell it!

Wǒ(1) bà(2)ba(3) mā(4)ma(5) hěn(6) máng(7). → Total: 7 Syllables!

The Four Tones & Pitch Scale

KLXHY Pg 3-4
⚠️ Teacher's Essential Command: Be DRAMATIC!
Chinese is a tonal language. Changing the pitch changes the meaning completely (e.g., mā = mother vs. mà = scold). Use hand gestures to draw the tone contours in the air, exaggerate pitch highs and lows, and never murmur!
1st Tone ā
Pitch: 5-5

High & Flat: Sounds like a hospital ECG flatline monitor (BEEEEEP---). Kept high and steady.

2nd Tone á
Pitch: 3-5

Rising: Sounds genuinely confused or surprised, exactly like asking "Huh?!" or "What?!".

3rd Tone ǎ
Pitch: 2-1-4

Fall & Rise: Like a rollercoaster dipping into a valley and curving up, or nodding your chin down and up.

4th Tone à
Pitch: 5-1

Sharp Drop: Sounds angry, commanding ("Stop!"), or like being suddenly stepped on ("Ouch!").

Complete Spelling Rules

1. The Space-Saving Rule 🗜️ ("The Middle Letter Gets Squeezed Out!")

KLXHY Pg 4-5

When initials join with compound finals iou, uei, and uen, writing space is compressed and the middle letter (o or e) is squeezed out!

iou → -iu (Standalone: you) All examples: x + iou = xiū | l + iou = liù | n + iou = niú | j + iou = jiǔ | q + iou = qiú | m + iou = miù | d + iou = diū
uei → -ui (Standalone: wei) All examples: d + uei = duì | t + uei = tuī | g + uei = guī | k + uei = kuì | h + uei = huì | zh + uei = zhuī | ch + uei = chuī | sh + uei = shuǐ | r + uei = ruì | z + uei = zuì | c + uei = cuì | s + uei = suì

2. The "Y & W" Bodyguards (Zero Initial Syllables) 🛡️

KLXHY Pg 4

Syllables cannot start with naked i, u, ü. Only open-mouth a, o, e can stand alone without bodyguards (e.g., ā, è).

i Group:
Alone → add y (yi, yin, ying)
Mixed → replace with y (ia→ya, ian→yan, iou→you)
u Group:
Alone → add w (u→wu)
Mixed → replace with w (ua→wa, uan→wan, uei→wei)
ü Group:
Always add y AND drop two dots (ü→yu, üe→yue, üan→yuan, ün→yun)

3. Dropping Dots on "ü" 🎩

KLXHY Pg 4-5
Crucial Pronunciation Reminder: Although the two dots are written off after j, q, x, y, THE PRONUNCIATION STILL FULLY RETAINS THE TIGHT "ü" SOUND! It never becomes the English "oo".
ju (jü), qu (qü), xu (xü)
jue (jüe), que (qüe), xue (xüe)
juan (jüan), quan (qüan), xuan (xüan)
jun (jün), qun (qün), xun (xün)

*Why keep dots for n and l? Because n/l can pair with both 'u' and 'ü' (e.g., lù land vs lǜ green). Dots are mandatory here!

4. Who Gets the Tone Crown? 👑 (6 Complete Rules)

KLXHY Pg 5

The vowel mouth-aperture priority ladder dictates where tone marks sit:

a > o > e > i > u > ü
Rule 1: Single Vowel Mark sits directly on it: mā, bǐ, kù
Rule 2: Two Vowels Follow ladder priority: bǎi, guó, piào
Rule 3: Twin Twins (iu & ui) Crown sits on the LAST vowel: tuī, qiú
Rule 4: Three Vowels Crown sits on the MIDDLE vowel: guǎi, shuài
Rule 5: Dot-Free "i" Erase the dot when putting a crown over i: nǐ, yín
Rule 6: Neutral Tone Never add tone marks on neutral syllables: bàba, māma

5. The Syllable Slicer / Dividing Mark (') ✂️

Official Term: Dividing Mark (géyīn fúhào). Used when a syllable starting with a, o, e follows another syllable, preventing syllable boundary confusion:

Tiananmen → Tiān'ānmén

6. Word Glue & Capitalization ✍️

Syllables forming one meaningful word are glued together with no space. Capitalize sentence beginnings and proper nouns:

wǒ de peng you → Wǒ de péngyou.

2. Chinese Characters (Hànzì)

Strokes, Writing Mechanics, Confusing Strokes & Stroke Order KLXHY Pg 8-13

Core Principle: One Single Continuous Stroke

A stroke is written in ONE single continuous movement from start to finish without lifting your pen from the paper. In this course, we learn strokes and character components, not alphabetical spellings.

💡 Defining "zhé" (折) & The Naming Secret:

• Definition of zhé (折): It is a continuous turning or bending motion in one stroke without lifting your pen. (Not just a 90° angle, as slanted turns like piě zhé 𠃋 are acute/slanted!).

• Why is "héng zhé" NOT called "héng shù"? Why is "shù zhé" NOT called "shù héng"?
Because in Chinese calligraphy, a stroke is defined by one unbroken breath of ink. If you call it "héng shù", it sounds like two separate strokes (1st stroke héng, 2nd stroke shù). Because the pen turns mid-stroke in a single movement, that turning action itself is named zhé (Turn)! Hence: héng + zhé = héng zhé (𠃍).

👁️ Spot the Differences: All 5 Confusing Stroke Pairs!

1. Straight vs Slanted vs Curved Hooks:
• shù gōu (亅): Straight vertical stem, hooks up-left.
• xié gōu (㇂): Slanted diagonal stem, hook points straight up to 12 o'clock.
• wān gōu (㇁): Curving right-bracket belly shape, hooks up-left.
2. Sharp Turn vs Wide Angle:
• piě zhé (𠃋): Acute turning corner (like a slanted 'V'), ends with flat horizontal line.
• piě diǎn (𡿨): Wide obtuse angle, second segment continues falling down-right as a long dot.
3. Left Hook vs Right Lift:
• shù gōu (亅): Hook flicks to the LEFT.
• shù tí (𠄌): Lift flicks up to the RIGHT like striking a match!
4. Lift Up-Right vs Hook Down-Left:
• héng zhé tí (㇊): Turns down and then LIFTS up-right (radical 讠).
• héng zhé gōu (𠃌): Turns down and then HOOKS inward to the left (radical 刀).
5. Straight Slant vs Curved Flat Base:
• héng xié gōu (⺄): Slants diagonally straight down-right before hooking up (飞).
• héng zhé wān gōu (㇈): Turns down, glides across a smooth curved bottom base, then hooks up (九, 乙).

8 Rules for Writing Full Characters

KLXHY Pg 9

These govern complete characters in square grids:

  • 1. Horizontal before vertical (十, 木)
  • 2. Left-falling before right-falling (人, 大)
  • 3. From top to bottom (三, 劳)
  • 4. From left to right (川, 什)
  • 5. From outside to inside (月, 问)
  • 6. Fill inside before sealing frame (四, 国)
  • 7. Middle precedes the two sides (小, 水)
  • 8. Vertical passing middle comes last (手, 事)

Character Structure Types

KLXHY Pg 9
  • Single-Component: One solid unit, cannot be split (木, 马, 人, 月).
  • Left-Right: Two side-by-side components (好, 你, 认, 朋).
  • Top-Bottom: Stacked components (字, 早, 点, 学).
  • Triangle: Three components stacked in pyramid (品, 森).
  • Semi-Enclosed: 2 or 3 sides framed (进, 店, 问, 医, 句).
  • Fully Enclosed: All 4 sides surrounded (国, 四, 回, 园).

How to Say "2" in Chinese? (èr vs liǎng)

èr (二)
  • • The number itself for simple counting: yī, èr, sān...
  • • Mathematics calculations & phone numbers
  • • Ordinals (second floor: èr céng)
  • • In tens and ones place: shí'èr (12), èrshí (20)
liǎng (两)
  • • Used when indicating quantity with a Measure Word (e.g., liǎng ge rén = 2 people).
  • ⚠️ 总数一定只有 2 (The total quantity measured must strictly be only 2!)
  • • In currency: liǎng kuài (RM 2)
  • • In time hour: liǎng diǎn (2:00)

Multi-Digit Reading Rules: "líng" and "yī"

1. The "líng" Rule (x0x / Middle Zeros)

Any zero trapped in the middle of place units MUST be explicitly pronounced as líng:

5078 → wǔqiān líng qīshíbā
9103 → jiǔqiān yībǎi líng sān
60240 → liùwàn líng èrbǎi sìshí
35001 → sānwàn wǔqiān líng yī

2. The "yī" Rule (x1x / Tens Digit 1 in Large Numbers)

When 11-19 appear inside hundreds or thousands, the digit yī CANNOT be omitted before shí:

3197 → sānqiān yībǎi jiǔshíqī
4815 → sìqiān bābǎi yīshíwǔ
81126 → bāwàn yīqiān yībǎi èrshíliù
79411 → qīwàn jiǔqiān sìbǎi yīshíyī

3. Hundreds + Tens: Direct Translation

250 → liǎngbǎi wǔshí / èrbǎi wǔshí

The Universal Chinese Number Formula

Number (Nu) + Unit / Measure Word (M) (+ Noun)

Applied across Currency, Time, Quantities, and Dates.

🚨 CRITICAL EXCEPTION: Days of the week do NOT follow this format!

Days of the week use xīngqī + Nu (e.g., xīngqīyī = Monday) because Nu + xīngqī indicates the quantity of weeks (e.g., yī xīngqī = one week)!

5. Grammar Focus

Syntax Architecture, Visual Combination Matrices, Particles & Sandhi KLXHY Pg 20-22

1. Chinese Sentence Structure (S + V + O & S + adv + A)

💡 NO Tense Conjugation in Chinese! (Past vs Present vs Future)

Unlike English, which alters verbs heavily (eat / ate / eaten or go / went / gone), Chinese verbs NEVER change form! Tense is conveyed entirely by time words or situational context:

Yesterday: Wǒ zuótiān qù túshūguǎn.
Today: Wǒ jīntiān qù túshūguǎn.
Tomorrow: Wǒ míngtiān qù túshūguǎn.

*Notice the verb "qù" remains 100% unchanged in all three sentences!

Sentence Structure in Questions:

Chinese statements and question sentences follow the EXACT same S + V + O word order! You do not invert subject and auxiliary verbs like English. Simply insert the question word into the object slot, or attach ma at the end!

3. The "hěn" (很) Rule & "yě" Sequence (S + adv + A)

Why does "Wǒ hěn hǎo" need "hěn"?

In Chinese, an Adjective directly following a Subject creates an awkward comparative tension (saying "Wǒ hǎo" feels like unfinished contrast: "I am fine, but someone else is not"). Therefore, a neutral structural adverb like hěn is inserted as a grammatical bridge, even when you do not literally mean "extremely/very"!

Sequence Order: 先 "yě" 后 "hěn" (S + yě + hěn + A)

When combining yě (also) and hěn (very), yě MUST ALWAYS precede hěn:

Wǒ yě hěn hǎo.  (NEVER: Wǒ hěn yě hǎo ❌)

Linguistic Reason: hěn forms an inseparable descriptive predicate unit with the Adjective ([hěn hǎo]), whereas yě is a sentence-level adverb modifying the entire state/action!

Subject (S) Adverb 1 (Scope) Adverb 2 (Degree Bridge) Adjective (A)
Wǒ / Tā / Lǎoshī yě (also) hěn (very) hǎo (good) / máng (busy)

Question Particles: "ma" vs "ne"

ma (吗) - Yes/No Question

Appended to a complete statement to turn it into a binary confirmation question:

Nǐ zài dàxué ma? (Are you at the university?)

ne (呢) - Bounce-Back Question

Keywords: Reciprocal follow-up / "What about...? / How about...?". Used to bounce the question back without repeating the sentence:

Wǒ hěn hǎo, nǐ ne? (I'm fine, and you?)

5W1H Question Words vs "ma"

🚨 GOLDEN RULE: NEVER Combine 5W1H Words with "ma"!

If a sentence already contains a 5W1H question word like nǎr / nǎlǐ (where) or shénme (what), DO NOT attach "ma" at the end!

✓ Correct: Nǐ qù nǎr?
✗ Wrong: Nǐ qù nǎr ma?
✓ Correct: Nǐ chī shénme?
✗ Wrong: Nǐ chī shénme ma?

Tone Sandhi Rules for "bù" (不)

Textbook Pg 36
Normal 4th Tone (bù): Before 1st, 2nd, and 3rd tone syllables:
• bù chī (1st tone)
• bù huí (2nd tone)
• bù xǐhuan (3rd tone)
Changes to 2nd Tone (bú): When followed by a 4th tone syllable:
• bú zài (4th tone)
• bú qù (4th tone)
• bú yào (4th tone)
• bú kèqi (4th tone)
V-not-V Neutral Tone (bu): In affirmative-negative question forms, "bu" is pronounced light and neutral:
• yào bu yào
• chī bu chī
• qù bu qù

Authentic Everyday Chinese Greetings Culture

"Nǐ hǎo" vs "Nǐ hǎo ma?" & The Copy-and-Paste Rule

• When greeting people in Chinese, you ONLY answer with "Wǒ hěn hǎo" if you hear the question particle "ma" (Nǐ hǎo ma?)!
• If someone says "Nǐ hǎo", do NOT say "Wǒ hěn hǎo"! You simply COPY & PASTE their exact words back: "Nǐ hǎo!".
• This Copy & Paste rule applies across all time greetings and farewells:

Zǎoshang hǎo! → Zǎoshang hǎo!
Xiàwǔ hǎo! → Xiàwǔ hǎo!
Wǎnshang hǎo! → Wǎnshang hǎo!
Zàijiàn! → Zàijiàn!

*Common everyday informal greetings also include: Qù nǎr? (Where are you heading?) and Chī le ma? (Have you eaten?).
