📐 How to Count Chinese Syllables & Words:

In Chinese, 1 Hanzi Character = 1 Spoken Syllable = 1 Pinyin Unit. Every single syllable you speak counts as exactly 1 syllable, regardless of the number of letters in its spelling:

Wǒ(1) bà(2)ba(3) mā(4)ma(5) hěn(6) máng(7). → Total: 7 Syllables!

The Four Tones & Pitch Scale

KLXHY Pg 3-4
⚠️ Teacher's Command: Be DRAMATIC!
Chinese is a tonal language. Different tones express completely different meanings (e.g., mā = mother vs. mà = scold). Use hand gestures to draw the tone contours in the air and exaggerate pitch highs and lows!
1st Tone ā
Pitch: 5-5

High & Flat: Sounds like a hospital ECG flatline monitor (BEEEEEP---). Kept steady.

2nd Tone á
Pitch: 3-5

Rising: Sounds genuinely confused, like asking "Huh?!" or "What?!".

3rd Tone ǎ
Pitch: 2-1-4

Fall & Rise: Rollercoaster curve dipping down into the valley then bouncing up.

4th Tone à
Pitch: 5-1

Sharp Drop: Sounds angry, like commanding "Stop!", or like being stepped on ("Ouch!").

2. The "Y & W" Bodyguards (Zero Initial Syllables) 🛡️

KLXHY Pg 4

Only big-mouth vowels a, o, e can stand alone (ā, è). Vowels i, u, ü require Y or W bodyguards:

i Group:
Alone → add y (yi, yin, ying)
Mixed → replace i with y (ia→ya, ian→yan)
u Group:
Alone → add w (u→wu)
Mixed → replace u with w (ua→wa, uan→wan)
ü Group:
Always add y AND drop two dots (ü→yu, üe→yue, üan→yuan, ün→yun)

4. Who Gets the Tone Crown? 👑 (6 Complete Rules)

KLXHY Pg 5

Priority ladder based on vowel mouth opening:

a > o > e > i > u > ü
Rule 1: Single Vowel Mark directly on it: mā, bǐ, kù
Rule 2: Two Vowels Follow ladder priority: bǎi, guó, piào
Rule 3: Twin Twins (iu & ui) Crown sits on the LAST vowel: tuī, qiú
Rule 4: Three Vowels Crown sits on the MIDDLE vowel: guǎi, shuài
Rule 5: Dot-Free "i" Erase dot when crowned: nǐ, yín
Rule 6: Neutral Tone No mark on neutral syllables: bàba, māma

5. The Syllable Slicer / Dividing Mark (') ✂️

Official Term: Dividing Mark. Used when a syllable starting with a, o, e follows another syllable, preventing syllable boundary confusion:

Tiananmen → Tiān'ānmén

6. Word Glue & Capitalization ✍️

Syllables forming one meaningful word are glued together with no space. Capitalize sentence beginnings and proper nouns:

wǒ de peng you → Wǒ de péngyou.

A stroke is written in ONE single continuous movement from start to finish without lifting your pen from the paper.

Strokes with Turns (zhé) (3)

KLXHY Pg 8, 10
💡 Defining "zhé" (折) & The Naming Secret:

• Definition: A turn (zhé) is a continuous change of direction without lifting your pen (not strictly a 90° corner, since piě zhé forms an acute/slanted turn!).
• Why is "héng zhé" NOT called "héng shù"? Why is "shù zhé" NOT called "shù héng"?
Because a stroke is defined by writing in ONE continuous movement without lifting pen from paper. Calling it "héng shù" would make it sound like two separate strokes. Because the stroke turns mid-way in a single action, that turning action itself is named zhé (Turn)! Hence: héng + zhé = héng zhé.

👁️ Spot the Differences: All 5 Confusing Stroke Pairs!

1. Straight vs Slanted vs Curved Hooks:
• shù gōu (亅): Straight vertical stem, hooks up-left.
• xié gōu (㇂): Slanted diagonal stem, hook points straight up to 12 o'clock.
• wān gōu (㇁): Curving right-bracket belly shape, hooks up-left.
2. Sharp Turn vs Wide Angle:
• piě zhé (𠃋): Acute turning corner (like a slanted 'V'), ends with flat horizontal line.
• piě diǎn (𡿨): Wide angle, second segment continues falling down-right as a long dot.
3. Left Hook vs Right Lift:
• shù gōu (亅): Hook flicks to the LEFT.
• shù tí (𠄌): Lift flicks up to the RIGHT like striking a match!
4. Lift Up-Right vs Hook Down-Left:
• héng zhé tí (㇊): Turns down and then LIFTS up-right.
• héng zhé gōu (𠃌): Turns down and then HOOKS inward to the left.
5. Straight Slant vs Curved Flat Base:
• héng xié gōu (⺄): Slants diagonally straight down-right before hooking up.
• héng zhé wān gōu (㇈): Turns down, glides across a smooth curved bottom base, then hooks up.

8 Rules for Writing Full Characters

KLXHY Pg 9
  • 1. Horizontal before vertical (十, 木)
  • 2. Left-falling before right-falling (人, 大)
  • 3. From top to bottom (三, 劳)
  • 4. From left to right (川, 什)
  • 5. From outside to inside (月, 问)
  • 6. Fill inside before sealing frame (四, 国)
  • 7. Middle precedes the two sides (小, 水)
  • 8. Vertical passing middle comes last (手, 事)

Character Structure Types

KLXHY Pg 9
  • Single-Component: One solid unit (木, 马, 人, 月).
  • Left-Right: Two side-by-side parts (好, 你, 认, 朋).
  • Top-Bottom: Stacked parts (字, 早, 点, 学).
  • Triangle: Pyramid structure (品, 森).
  • Semi-Enclosed: 2 or 3 sides framed (进, 店, 问, 医, 句).
  • Fully Enclosed: All 4 sides surrounded (国, 四, 回, 园).

How to Say "2" in Chinese? (èr vs liǎng)

èr
  • • The number itself for simple counting: yī, èr, sān...
  • • Mathematics equations & ID numbers
  • • Ordinals (second floor: èr céng)
  • • Tens and ones places: shí'èr (12), èrshí (20)
liǎng
  • • Used when indicating quantity with a Measure Word (e.g., liǎng ge rén).
  • ⚠️ The total quantity measured must strictly be only 2!
  • • In currency units: liǎng kuài (RM 2)
  • • In time hours: liǎng diǎn (2:00)

Multi-Digit Reading Rules: "líng" and "yī"

1. The "líng" Rule (x0x / Middle Zeros)

Any zero trapped in the middle of digits MUST be read as líng:

5078 → wǔqiān líng qīshíbā
9103 → jiǔqiān yībǎi líng sān
60240 → liùwàn líng èrbǎi sìshí

2. The "yī" Rule (x1x / Tens Digit 1 in Larger Numbers)

When tens digit is 1 inside numbers ≥ 100, the digit yī MUST be pronounced before shí:

3197 → sānqiān yībǎi jiǔshíqī
4815 → sìqiān bābǎi yīshíwǔ
81126 → bāwàn yīqiān yībǎi èrshíliù

3. Hundreds + Tens: Direct Translation

The Universal Chinese Number Formula

Number (Nu) + Unit / Measure Word (M) (+ Noun)
🚨 CRITICAL EXCEPTION: ONLY Days of the Week do NOT follow this format!

Days of the week use xīngqī + Nu (xīngqīyī = Monday). Using Nu + xīngqī expresses the quantity of weeks (e.g. yī xīngqī = one week)!

Visual Matrix, Syntax Architecture, Sandhi & Question Particle Systems KLXHY Pg 20-22

1. Chinese Sentence Structure (S + V + O & S + adv + A)

💡 NO Verb Conjugations in Chinese! (Past vs Present vs Future)

Unlike English, which constantly changes verb forms (eat / ate / eaten), Chinese verbs NEVER change form! Tenses are indicated simply by adding time words:

Yesterday: Wǒ zuótiān qù túshūguǎn.
Today: Wǒ jīntiān qù túshūguǎn.
Tomorrow: Wǒ míngtiān qù túshūguǎn.

*The verb "qù" remains 100% identical in all tenses!

Questions Follow the Exact Same Word Order:

Chinese statements and questions share the identical S + V + O architecture! No flipping auxiliary verbs. Simply attach ma at the end, or place the question word directly in the object slot!

2. Time Word Position: S + Time + V + O

In Chinese, time words MUST be placed BEFORE the verb. When multiple time units appear simultaneously, they must strictly follow the Chinese logical order: LARGEST unit to SMALLEST unit (Day → Time of Day → Exact Clock Time):

3. Wǒ míngtiān zǎoshang bā diǎn qù túshūguǎn. (...tomorrow morning at 8:00.)

3. The "hěn" Rule & "yě" Sequence (S + adv + A)

Why do we say "Wǒ hěn hǎo" instead of "Wǒ hǎo"?

In Chinese, directly saying "Wǒ hǎo" sounds awkward and incomplete, as if you are trying to compare yourself against others ("I am good, but you aren't"). The word hěn acts as a harmless structural bridge connecting the subject to the adjective.

Word Order: "yě" precedes "hěn" (S + yě + hěn + A)

Simple intuitive rule: hěn glues directly to the adjective to form one single description unit ([hěn hǎo] = doing fine). yě (also) applies to the whole person, so it must stand in front:

Wǒ yě hěn hǎo.  (Never: Wǒ hěn yě hǎo ❌)

Subject (S) Adverb 1 (Scope) Adverb 2 (Degree Bridge) Adjective (A)
Wǒ / Tā / Lǎoshī yě (also) hěn (very / structural) hǎo (good / fine)

Question Particles: ma vs ne

ma - Yes/No Question

Simply take any normal sentence and add "ma?" at the very end to turn it into a yes/no question!

Nǐ zài dàxué ma? (Are you at the university?)

ne - Bounce-Back Question

The "What about you?" shortcut! Instead of repeating the entire sentence, just say [Person] + ne? to bounce the same question right back:

Wǒ hěn hǎo, nǐ ne? (I'm fine, what about you?)

5W1H Question Words vs "ma"

🚨 NEVER Combine 5W1H Words with "ma"!

If a sentence already contains a question word like nǎr / nǎlǐ (where) or shénme (what), DO NOT add "ma" at the end!

✓ Correct: Nǐ qù nǎr?
✗ Wrong: Nǐ qù nǎr ma?
✓ Correct: Nǐ chī shénme?
✗ Wrong: Nǐ chī shénme ma?

Tone Sandhi Rules for "bù"

Textbook Pg 36
Meaning of "bù": "bù" means "no" or "not", placed directly in front of verbs or adjectives to make them negative.
Normal 4th Tone (bù): Before 1st, 2nd, and 3rd tone syllables:
• bù chī (1st tone)
• bù huí (2nd tone)
• bù xǐhuan (3rd tone)
Changes to 2nd Tone (bú): Before another 4th tone syllable:
• bú zài (4th tone)
• bú qù (4th tone)
• bú yào (4th tone)
• bú kèqi (4th tone)
V-not-V Neutral Tone (bu): In choice questions, "bu" is light & neutral:
• yào bu yào
• chī bu chī
• qù bu qù
